04Where the dollar goes

Every cost is a peak on the same trace.

Read the operating statement the way Waters reads a chromatogram: as a sequence of peaks, sized by how much of the molecule — or in this case, the dollar — is detected at each step. Cost of revenue is the dominant peak. Everything else is a smaller resolution off the same baseline.

Add the peaks: 74¢ in costs, leaving an operating margin of 25.4% on operating income of $802.59M. The flat baseline between peaks is where the profit lives.

05Three continents, evenly

No region carries the company. Every region does.

Waters is one of the most geographically balanced large-cap labs companies in the U.S. — the Americas, Europe, and Asia-Pacific each take roughly a third of revenue. When one continent's pharma capex slows, another keeps buying.

06The bet · BD biosciences

In early 2026, Waters bought itself a second act.

Waters completed a transformative merger with the life-science and diagnostics business spun out of Becton, Dickinson — a deliberate move out of pure analytical instrumentation and into diagnostics and discovery. Stack it alongside the 2023 Wyatt Technology tuck-in, and a pattern emerges: organic growth first, bolt-on M&A second, buybacks third.

07The bench next door

Three rivals share the lab. One of them dwarfs the room.

Waters' chromatograph sits on the same bench as Agilent's, Mettler-Toledo's, and Thermo Fisher's. The first two compete head-to-head on instruments. The third is a different scale of animal — five times Waters' size and growing into the same workflows.

Waters is not in the business of selling drugs. It is in the business of proving them. A 68-year-old Massachusetts company, sitting on the bench next to every approved molecule, taking twenty cents off the top of a $3.17B year.

The next chapter — diagnostics and discovery via the BD merger — is a bet that the bench gets longer. The ones before it (Wyatt; the consumables flywheel; the 100-country sales force) suggest Waters knows how to make the bench pay.
