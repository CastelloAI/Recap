The business · two engines

Regulated meter.
Open prairie.

NEE runs two distinct businesses on the same balance sheet. FPL, the rate-regulated Florida utility, prints predictable cash from six million customer accounts. NEER, the competitive arm, builds wind, solar, nuclear, and storage across the continent — and sells it forward on long-dated contracts.

Where the money goes · the bill

A dollar of revenue,
disassembled.

Of every dollar that flows in, the largest slice — twenty-eight cents — leaves immediately to buy fuel and purchased power. The rest funds the towers, the turbines, the bondholders, and the tax bill, before anything makes it back to shareholders.

Twenty cents remain — the room from which interest gets paid down further, capex gets funded, and the dividend gets cut.

The weight · capital structure

Building the grid
means borrowing the grid.

Utility infrastructure is one of the most capital-intensive games on the board. NEE carries $95.6B of total debt against $66.5B of equity — a 144% debt-to-equity ratio that mirrors the asset base it has been quietly assembling for a hundred years.

Free cash flow is, by design, negative. Every dollar earned — and many borrowed — is being redeployed into rate base, renewables, and the dividend. The bet is that the asset side compounds faster than the liability side.

One playbook,
three pockets.

The capital story is direct: regulated rate-base growth at FPL, renewable project development at NEER, and a defended dividend that keeps the equity story intact. Capital recycling — asset sales and tax-equity partnerships — funds the rest.

The page opened on a thesis of scale. It closes on a thesis of flow. NEE's century-deep franchise compounds at the asset side; the dividend — 68.48% of trailing earnings — is what flows back to the shareholders funding the next mile of wire.
