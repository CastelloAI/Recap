Interlude · I

Two companies merged in 2020. The result is one machine — pulling air through a compressor, pushing thrust the world is willing to pay for.

The machine · 03

Three stages of compression. $88.60B of thrust.

Air enters at Pratt & Whitney, passes through Collins, exits through Raytheon — three segments that, together, produced FY '25 revenue of $88.60B. The mix is remarkably balanced: no single segment carries more than 37% of the load.

Interlude · II

A dollar enters at the top of the engine. Nine-tenths of it never makes it past combustion.

A hundred cents in · 05

Of every dollar of revenue, 10.5¢ survives as operating income.

Cost of revenue alone consumes 80 cents of every dollar — the cost of building jet engines, missile systems, and avionics at industrial scale. R&D, SG&A, and intangible amortization take the rest. What's left is the operating margin that funds the company.

Interlude · III

Capital is moving from buybacks to bays. With Washington watching defense returns, the dollars head into production lines instead.

The bet · 07

Interlude · IV

In an industry of three, customers and rivals share a runway. Boeing buys engines from Pratt — and competes against Raytheon downrange.

The competition · 08

Three peers. One is also a customer.

GE's engines collide with Pratt's on every Airbus and Boeing narrowbody. Boeing buys from Collins and Pratt — and goes head to head with Raytheon on missile programs. Honeywell shares the cockpit and the avionics rack with Collins.

The takeaway · 09

We opened with three segments and one spine. We close at the residual — what survives after a year of building.

10.5¢ survives.

Of every dollar that enters the engine, ten and a half cents come out as operating income. The rest is the cost of building the most reliable jet engines, missiles, and avionics the modern world will pay for.
