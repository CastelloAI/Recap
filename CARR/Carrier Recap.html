Interlude · 02

But cooling something costs something. A hundred cents in. Not all of them survive.

The bill · 100¢ in

The thermostat
reads 100.

For every dollar of revenue, here is where the heat goes — operating costs, the long shadow of Viessmann amortization, and the interest on the debt that bought it.

What's left — 53¢ — pays for everything else: taxes, the dividend, buybacks, and the next bet. Net 6.8¢ survives.

Interlude · 03

And then, in 2023, they bought a German heat-pump giant for thirteen billion dollars.

The bet · Viessmann · ~$13B

A heavy pan,
and a long lever.

Carrier put $11.83B of debt on the scale to pull $13B of European heat-pump leadership across. Equity ($14.13B) holds the other side. The balance is intentional.

Total debt $11.83B Most of it Viessmann. ~$7.6B in goodwill on the books from the deal alone.
Equity $14.13B $1.55B cash on hand. New $2.5B revolver. No major maturities until 2027.

And the cash that comes off the top — $2.51B operating, $2.12B free — funded $772M in dividends and an active buyback. Management is targeting $1.5B more in repurchases for 2026.

The takeaway · what survives

A dollar of conditioned air,
six and four-fifths cents
of profit.

That is the bottom of Willis Carrier's century-old machine: $1.48B of net income on $21.75B of revenue. The rest goes to building, buying, refinancing, and refilling the next room with cold air.

100¢ in 6.8¢ out

One hundred and ten years on, the math is simple. Move the heat. Bill twice — once for the box, once for the upkeep. Reinvest before the room warms up again.
