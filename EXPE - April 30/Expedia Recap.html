— ON THE BUSINESS
02 · The business·How a dollar arrives

Three pipes,
one watermark.

Expedia is paid three different ways for the same act — somebody books a room. As merchant of record, it takes the money first and pays the hotel later. As an agent, it just clips a commission. And the platform itself sells shelf space: ads, placements, sponsored search.

06 · The bet·Capital, allocated

$3.11B of free cash flow.
One question: where to send it.

Expedia carries $4.47B in debt against $1.28B in equity — a leveraged frame, propped up by $5.41B in cash and a $2.5B revolver it isn’t drawing on. The cash spigot pays for three things: tech that has to keep working, dividends that just came back, and shares it keeps buying back.

— THE TAKEAWAY
08 · The takeaway·What survives the dollar

Out of every booked dollar,
fifteen cents make it home.

Open the page on a search box. Close it on a ledger. Expedia takes $14.73B in revenue, sends nearly half of it back into the marketing machine, pays its hosts, its engineers, the taxman — and what survives is the operating margin: 12.7 cents on the dollar, before the bill from the balance sheet.

The thesis hasn’t changed since 1996: be the place a search becomes a stay. Two-thirds of bookings still arrive as merchant of record — money first, room later. Twenty-two cents from agency commissions. Ten from advertising. The rest, a three-billion-dollar river of free cash flow that pays the dividend, retires the share count, and waits for the next thing to buy.
