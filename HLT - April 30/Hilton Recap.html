● The thesis · 9,100 keys

9,100 hotels. 143 countries. One night at a time.

Hilton is not a real-estate company. It is a brand on the doorframe of someone else's building — and a fee that posts every time a guest swipes a key.

Conrad Hilton bought one Texas hotel in 1919. A century on, his heirs sell something stranger: permission to use the name.

● The business · Asset-light

Hilton holds the keys.
Other people hold the buildings.

Two segments. The big one — management & franchise — collects fees from third-party owners flying the flag. The small one runs hotels Hilton actually owns. Of every dollar of revenue, 66¢ is just costs Hilton bills back to those owners and passes through.

● Where it goes · The bill

A guest checks out. The bill is itemised.

Hilton's cost structure looks unusual because so much of it isn't really Hilton's. Reimbursed owner costs are dollars the company invoices on behalf of franchisees and pays back out — they show up on both sides of the income statement and they dominate.

Net of pass-through, the company keeps ~22.4¢ on the dollar.

Strip out reimbursements and Hilton looks like what it is: a licensing & loyalty business with a small owned-hotel tail. The rest of the income statement — D&A, overhead, interest — fits in a single column.

You don't run a 9,100-hotel franchise without leverage. The buildings sit on someone else's books — but the debt sits on Hilton's.

● The weight · $11.6B in debt

Asset-light is balance-sheet-heavy.

Hilton runs at negative book equity — minus $5.39B. That's not distress. It's the math of a company that has spent a decade returning capital to shareholders while financing operations with senior unsecured notes and a term loan.
