The business · how the dollar arrives

Eighty-nine cents in tickets. Eleven in jet fuel sold to someone else.

The Airline segment — passenger tickets, premium cabins, cargo, baggage, and seat upgrades — does most of the carrying. Premium and loyalty alone, including the American Express co-brand, account for ~62% of airline revenue and have been the fastest-growing line. The Refinery — Monroe Energy, fully owned — sells fuel to the world and quietly hedges Delta's own tank.

Founded in 1924 as a crop-dusting outfit. A century later, the same company moves more than two hundred million people a year through Atlanta, Amsterdam, Heathrow, CDG, Incheon, Tokyo.

Every flight is a balance sheet in motion. Most of the dollar leaves before it ever lands.
The bill · where 77¢ goes

Most of the ticket pays for people and the fuel they're flying.

Six line-items take roughly seventy-seven cents of every revenue dollar. Salaries lead. Fuel follows. Regional carriers and contracted services come third. The remainder — depreciation, selling, maintenance — fills out the tail.

The footprint · where it flies

Seventy percent of revenue still lives at home.

Atlanta is the heart. Then the Atlantic — Heathrow, CDG, Amsterdam — then Latin America and the Pacific reach. The international book is bigger than most carriers can claim, but the dollar still answers to the United States.

To fly that map, the company once borrowed deep. The runway since has been about climbing out.
The bet · balance-sheet first

Pay it down. Buy new metal. Stay investment-grade.

Adjusted net debt fell by $3.7 billion in 2025 to ~$14.3B; gross leverage tightened to 2.4×. Capex of $4.3B went into fleet modernization and lounge expansion. The dividend is small — $440M — and buybacks remain limited. The company is choosing the balance sheet first.
